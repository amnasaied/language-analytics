| Feature | Free R tool | Source (paper) | What the paper found | How it relates to our question |
| :---- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Sentiment polarity \+ incongruity** (positive & negative words in the same comment; polarity-flip count) | `tidytext` \+ Bing/AFINN lexicons | Riloff et al. (2013) [aclanthology.org/D13-1066](https://aclanthology.org/D13-1066/) | Found a dominant sarcasm form: a positive-sentiment word ("love", "enjoy") contrasted with a negative situation ("being ignored"). The positive/negative contrast itself was the key signal and boosted recognition recall. | This is the core discriminator  sarcastic comments pair opposite-polarity content in one utterance, while sincere ones stay consistent. We measure it as counts of polarity flips within a comment. |
| Explicit vs. implicit incongruity (\# opposite-polarity transitions, longest same-polarity run, \# positive/negative words) | `tidytext`  The author used pre-trained word embeddings (like Word2Vec or GloVe) to represent words as vectors and measured the "distance" between them using cosine similarity . They calculated two types of features for every sentence: Unweighted Similarity (S): They identified the most similar and most dissimilar word pairs in the sentence and used their maximum and minimum scores as features . Distance-weighted Similarity (WS): They took the similarity scores and divided them by the square of the linear distance between the two words in the sentence . This means that words appearing close together are given more importance than those far apart . By adding these "semantic discordance" features to existing models, they were able to improve sarcasm detection performance  | Joshi et al. (2016)  [arxiv.org/pdf/1610.00883](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.00883) | The author measures context incongruity by calculating the semantic similarity and discordance between pairs of words within the same sentence 1\. Is it the incongruity between sentiments in the same comment? Not exactly. While the author acknowledges that prior research (including their own previous work) focused heavily on sentiment-based incongruity—such as a positive verb followed by a negative situation—the goal of this specific paper is to detect sarcasm without relying on sentiment words . Instead of looking for conflicting "feelings" (positive vs. negative), they look for semantic discordance (words that don't usually belong together) . For example, in the sentence "A woman needs a man like a fish needs a bicycle," there are no sentiment-bearing words like "happy" or "bad" . The author captures the sarcasm by showing that "fish" and "bicycle" have a very low cosine similarity score (0.130), which serves as a "handle" for context incongruity  | Turns the incongruity idea into concrete, countable features that reliably separate sarcasm from non-sarcasm, all computable from a free R sentiment lexicon over the token sequence. |
| **Emotional incongruity** (co-present opposing emotions, e.g. joy \+ disgust) | `syuzhet` / `tidytext` \+ NRC EmoLex | Mohammad & Turney (2013)  [NRC Emotion Lexicon](http://saifmohammad.com/WebPages/NRC-Emotion-Lexicon.htm) | Built and validated a crowdsourced lexicon mapping words to 8 emotions and 2 polarities, shown to be reliable for emotion classification. | Sarcasm often mixes clashing emotions (positive-emotion words describing a negative event). Co-occurring opposing NRC emotions in one comment is a signal largely absent from sincere text. |
| **Emphasis-aware sentiment** (ALL CAPS, "\!\!\!", intensifiers, "but") | `vader` | Hutto & Gilbert (2014) [ojs.aaai.org/…/14550](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/14550) | A rule-based lexicon quantifying how capitalization, exclamation marks, degree modifiers, and contrastive conjunctions amplify or shift sentiment intensity; matched human raters on social-media text. | Sarcasm exaggerates false praise through emphasis. VADER converts these surface cues into numeric intensity features that separate exaggerated (often sarcastic) comments from plain sincere ones. |
| **Punctuation, quotation marks, emoticons, laughter tokens** | `quanteda` \+ `textfeatures` | González-Ibáñez et al. (2011) [aclanthology.org/P11-2102](https://aclanthology.org/P11-2102/) | Comparing sarcastic vs. sincere tweets, the most reliable manual cues for sarcasm were emoticons, laughter onomatopoeia (haha/lol), heavy punctuation (\!\!\!, ?\!), and quotation marks. | These are directly extractable typographic features; their elevated frequency shifts probability toward sarcasm — a low-cost, high-signal replacement for LIWC punctuation categories. |
| **Interjections / cue words** ("gee", "gosh", "wow") | `quanteda` dictionary (+ `udpipe` POS) | Kreuz & Caucci (2007) [aclanthology.org/W07-0101](https://aclanthology.org/W07-0101/) | In a reading experiment, the presence of interjections significantly predicted whether readers rated a statement as sarcastic; punctuation also contributed to perceived sarcastic intent. | Psycholinguistic evidence that specific lexical markers cue sarcasm perception, so counting interjections gives a feature grounded in how readers actually detect sarcasm. |
